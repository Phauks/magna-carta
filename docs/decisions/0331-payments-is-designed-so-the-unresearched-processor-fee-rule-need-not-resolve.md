**The unresearched rule is not researched here, not defaulted, and not blocking — because the design does not need it.**

**Context:** `@magna-carta/trust-jurisdictions` already anticipated this question. `TypedRequirements` carries `processorFees: { mayChargeClientPrincipal: boolean }`, and `REQUIREMENT_BUCKETS` places it in `data` — the bucket whose members may never resolve from silence, because a wrong silence there asserts a wrong fact about the world rather than merely over-complying. Nevada's profile records it as `GAP`, the state that means nobody looked, which is the opposite type from `NOT_ADDRESSED` and is never resolvable. The note is specific about what was searched: NRPC 1.15, SCR 78, SCR 216-221 and the State Bar of Nevada's trust-accounting handbook, for five terms, with no hit addressing how a card-processing fee interacts with a trust deposit. Nevada also ships with `attorneyReview: null`, so every requirement on it currently refuses with `jurisdiction-not-verified` in any case.

The wider corpus is consistent about the principle and thin about the authority: "processing fees must not be charged against client principal, and chargebacks must not invade other clients' funds" recurs across the fifty-six jurisdictions, marked `[KNOWLEDGE]` or `Unknown / needs verification` in the large majority. Four jurisdictions address it expressly, and they diverge — Arizona permits the lawyer's own funds in trust to pay merchant fees and offset chargebacks outright; Texas and Maine instead permit a non-trust transit account with a two-business-day transfer window, Texas adding a three-business-day reimbursement duty for any chargeback shortfall. So there is no single answer to inherit, and picking one would be the platform legislating.

**Decision:**

(1) **Implement only the arrangement that is safe under every possible answer.** The fee is borne by the firm, debited from its operating account, and never reduces client principal. Under `mayChargeClientPrincipal: false` that is the only permitted arrangement; under `true` it is still permitted, because a firm is free to absorb a cost it could pass on. The permission the field would grant is therefore never exercised, and the field does not have to resolve for the subsystem to ship.

(2) **Refuse by name every arrangement that would need it.** Netting a fee out of a trust deposit (already refused structurally by the composition guard); a merchant-fee cushion inside a trust account on the Arizona model (unrepresentable — the second door's category is closed at two kinds); a transit account with a transfer window on the Texas or Maine model (not designed). Each refusal names the missing research rather than the missing feature, so the fix is legible: research the field for the jurisdiction, quote the rule verbatim, have an attorney review the profile.

(3) **Do not resolve the field, and do not let this design's existence imply it was resolved.** A payments subsystem that ships without touching client principal has not answered the question and must not be read as having answered it. The `GAP` stands.

(4) **Surcharge is a different question and needs a different field.** `processorFees` is about client principal in trust; surcharging an operating invoice payment is not covered by it, and `TypedRequirements` has no member for surcharge legality at all. That field belongs in the `restriction` bucket rather than `data` — a wrong silence there hands a lawyer a practice a state bans, so it resolves to off only on a silence recorded as read — and it needs fifty-six jurisdictions of primary-source research plus card-network registration before anything is built on it.

**Consequences:** the platform can accept a trust-destined electronic payment without any jurisdiction fact resolving, and the honest reason is stated rather than glossed. Three lawful arrangements the corpus records stay unbuilt with their preconditions named. This inherits the trust corpus's posture — a jurisdiction fact refuses rather than falls back, and a firm may be stricter than its jurisdiction while the platform may never be stricter on its behalf — and applies it to a subsystem outside trust. Open questions are flags K2, K3 and K6 in `docs/specs/2026-08-15-payments-design.md`. Builds on **ADR-0009** (the refusing jurisdiction posture) and **ADR-0032**.
