**Home-realm discovery is a fact about a domain, resolved on submit, and it never says anything about the address that was typed.**

**Context:** A firm has an identity provider and its member types a password. Silently refusing sends them to a password reset that either does nothing (confusing) or does something (a live password on a federated account — the second door the firm bought single sign-on to close). Silently allowing gives the firm a revocation point that does not revoke: somebody dismissed on Monday still signs in on Tuesday with a credential the provider has never heard of. Resolving as the address loses focus is the third obvious answer and the worst, because it lets an anonymous prober learn that a named firm is a customer here — for a legal practice's client and vendor lists, sometimes the confidential fact itself. The shipped sign-in screen already anticipated this: it resolves on submit and never on blur, leaves the password field visible but inert, and says the typed password was not checked.

**Decision:** Before either the address or the password is checked, the submitted address's **domain** is resolved against a verified-domain map, answering exactly two shapes. **`continue`** proceeds into the ordinary credential path with its single neutral outcome set. **`redirect`** carries the provider's display name and start URL; the password is **discarded unread** and **no ladder rung is spent**, because nothing was tested. This is not an oracle because the map is keyed on a **verified** domain, and verification is a `_magna-carta-challenge.<domain>` DNS TXT record the domain's owner published — anyone can already resolve it, so the firm chose to make the fact public in the only way the platform accepts. That is precisely why **the map may only ever be populated by domain verification and never by an administrator pasting a list of addresses**: an address list would make discovery a fact about people. Two consequences are stated rather than left to be discovered: the answer must be **identical for a verified domain with no live accounts at all**, since discovery is about the domain; and a `redirect` costing no rung while a `continue` may cost one is an observable difference that is fine, being the same public domain-level fact by another route. A claim on a well-known consumer mail domain is **refused outright** against a maintained deny-list, because one firm federating `@gmail.com` would redirect every unrelated visitor whose address happens to sit there. Enforcement is **`optional | preferred | exclusive`**; `exclusive` **removes** the password method from the register for every member rather than ignoring it, and cannot be turned on until the firm's Owner holds an enrolled **passkey**.

**Considered & rejected:** _resolving on blur or as the address is typed_ (an anonymous customer-list oracle, and the reason the shipped screen resolves on submit); _checking the password anyway and discarding the result_ (a live credential on a federated account, and a timing signal); _keying discovery on the address_ (makes it a fact about a person, and an administrator-supplied address list would be exactly that); _trusting the plugin's `domain` field unverified_ (it stores a `domainVerified` flag and verifies nothing; an unverified claim on a competitor's domain would redirect their staff to an attacker's provider); _`exclusive` ignoring the password rather than removing it_ (an ignored credential can still be reset, phished, and later found working after a mode change); _`exclusive` with no break-glass_ (a provider outage, a misconfigured claim or a departed vendor would lock a firm out of its own practice-management system, and no amount of protocol correctness makes that acceptable); _break-glass as a platform-held emergency password_ (the firm asked the platform not to hold a password; a passkey is phishing-resistant, bound to hardware the Owner holds, and is not the identity provider); _adopting SCIM_ (a whole provisioning protocol, when the firm's own per-firm `member` deactivation already strips every permission at the read path and the residual gap — the interval between revocation and the next sign-in — is bounded by a federated session cap instead).

**Consequences:** `firm_identity_provider` carries the firm, the protocol, the domain (unique platform-wide, first verified claim wins), the verification instant, the enforcement mode and the display name; a domain whose TXT record disappears stops redirecting and falls back to `continue` rather than locking the firm out. `provisionUserOnEveryLogin` is on and a federated session is capped (owner flag **F11**, recommended twelve hours idle and seven days absolute). The provider's own errors are never relayed to the caller; an assertion the platform will not accept produces the platform's one neutral sentence. Uses `@better-auth/sso` (MIT, a separate package) for the protocol half only. Builds on **ADR-0004** (first-party auth), **ADR-0280** (the neutral-answer posture on the joining surface), **ADR-0068** (the reserved absent answer).
