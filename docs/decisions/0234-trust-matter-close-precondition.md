**A trust balance blocks a matter close, and the block is proven inside the close batch rather than by the read in front of it.**

**Context:** The matter shell has carried a named close hook since the lifecycle landed, and it has been empty the whole time: the rule that wanted it — a matter cannot close while its client trust ledger holds money — could not be written until there was a movement surface with balances and holds to read. There now is one. Two things had to be settled before the rule could be a rule rather than a warning: who registers it, and what makes it true at the moment the matter actually closes.

**Decision:** (1) **Hard block, no override.** A matter with a non-zero client trust balance, or with an active hold on that ledger, is refused. The precedent nearby went the other way — a custody packet linked to a matter is an advisory notice, on the reasoning that a blocking gate teaches firms to unlink rather than to resolve — and trust is the case where that reasoning inverts. There is no equivalent of unlinking: the money is in the account whether or not the matter is closed, and the matter closing is what stops anyone looking at it. The refusal names the balance, the trust account holding it, the portion under an active hold, and the two movements that legitimately empty a client ledger — a disbursement to the client, or a transfer of earned fees to the operating account. A refusal that reports a state without naming its exits leaves the reader to guess which operation on the screen is the lawful one, and in trust the unlawful guesses are the expensive ones.

(2) **A matter with no trust ledger closes freely, and the gate never creates one to find out.** Most matters never hold client money and own no leaf; the absence of a ledger is the ordinary case, not a condition to refuse.

(3) **The rules are supplied by the composition root, not imported by the matter shell.** The hook is a contract — a `check` that reports failures and an optional guard — and `@magna-carta/matters` ships an empty registry it never fills. The app wires trust's rule in. The alternative, a matter package that imports the trust package so the registry can be populated statically, would make the gate unbypassable at the cost of the matter lifecycle depending on the financial core, and of every consumer of the matter shell compiling trust. The direction chosen keeps the shell ignorant of money and pays for it with one wiring line and a route test that fails if the wiring is ever dropped.

(4) **The refusal is proven at commit, not only read before it.** D1 has no interactive transaction, so the window between the check's read and the close's commit is real, and a trust deposit is exactly the writer that can land in it. A precondition may therefore contribute a guard statement that rides the close batch and re-asserts its condition there. The guard's defining property is that it can never store anything: trust's aims an unrepresentable `kind` at exactly those matter leaves that still hold money or carry an active hold, so it matches no rows when the ledger is clear and violates the node table's own CHECK when it is not. A violated constraint aborts the whole batch, which is what makes this the correct shape rather than a guarded `UPDATE` matching zero rows: a no-op close would still have committed its audit row, its `matter.closed` event, and an idempotency record whose replay would report a close that never happened. The service then re-runs the checks and reports what the guard caught, so the caller receives the same actionable refusal it would have received a moment earlier rather than a constraint message.

**Consequences:** The close operation answers a refusal with its own problem type carrying each rule that refused, rather than a generic validation failure. The first-mover cost is paid for whatever comes next: a custody rule, a billing rule, or an unbilled-work rule registers the same way and, if it has the same race exposure, guards the same way. This settles the close question only. What a firm does with money it cannot return — the unclaimed-funds and escheat obligations, which vary by jurisdiction in destination, dormancy period, and reclaimability — remains unbuilt and unencoded, which means the refusal deliberately does not offer it as a third way out.
