**Notification retention is a firm setting bounded at thirty and three hundred and sixty-five days, and the nightly sweep tallies the unread it takes rather than dropping the fact — surfaced beside the live unread count, and cleared by the same act that marks the feed read.**

**Context:** The delivery design settled that a notification is a POINTER and never a record — the source row, the audit log, and the outbox row are the record, each with its own retention posture set by its own subsystem — and that this is what makes a deletion policy defensible in a subsystem where the custody register refuses one outright: the custodian rule is about a client's irreplaceable property, and this is about a badge. It recommended a fixed platform default of ninety days from occurrence, read or unread, and left a firm-tier override as a later slice. Two things then needed settling before the record could be built rather than after: whether the window is the platform's or the firm's, and what happens to a member who was away while their unread items aged out. The second question is the one with a bad default: a badge of zero is not a neutral answer, it is the sentence "there is nothing you missed", and the platform has just deleted the only rows that could have contradicted it.

**Decision:**

1. **The window is the firm's, inside declared bounds, from the first migration.** `notifications.retentionDays`, an integer in `[30, 365]`, defaulting to 90, refused at the WRITE rather than clamped. The bounds are not decoration. Below thirty days a member returning from two weeks' leave opens an empty feed, which is the failure the feature exists to avoid. Above a year the feed is an unbounded read, and — the argument that actually binds — every extra day is another day a pointer outlives the access check that justified writing it. A firm asking for five years is asking for the wrong instrument: the record it wants is the matter timeline and the audit log, which keep their own retention and are untouched by this sweep. Firm-level rather than per-member, because retention is a records-management decision a firm makes once, and a per-member window would have two people disagreeing about whether the same notification still exists.

2. **The sweep tallies before it deletes, and the tally is per member.** One bounded pass reads, for the events it is about to remove, how many of each member's recipient rows were still `unread` and the oldest occurrence among them; both the tally upsert and the deletes commit in ONE batch, because a crash between them would lose the tally and leave the member with nothing but a zero. The tally accumulates across passes — a member away for two months is swept on several nights — and its oldest instant only ever moves backward, because how far back a gap reaches is a fact about the whole gap and not about the last pass that touched it.

3. **The unread count reports the two numbers SEPARATELY and never adds them.** `unreadCount` is work waiting; `sweptUnreadCount` (with the oldest instant behind it) is work that aged out. Summing them would produce a number that means neither, and a badge a member could not act on: the swept items have no rows left to open. What they point back at — the matter timeline, the deadline itself, the audit log — is all still there, which is the whole reason deleting the pointer was acceptable.

4. **Clearing the tally is the same act as marking the feed read**, and rides the same cursor bound. The tally is by construction older than every live row a page can show, so a cursor-bounded "I have dealt with the backlog I was shown" covers it exactly. No second route, no second gesture, and — the failure this avoids — no badge a member has no way to clear.

5. **It is a TALLY, not a copy.** A count and two instants per member, and nothing about which notifications they were: no kind, no category, no subject, no matter. A swept-unread record that named what it had swept would reintroduce, in a table nothing filters, precisely the rows the retention sweep had just removed — and would outlive the access check that justified them by exactly as long as the tally lives.

**Considered and rejected:** *a fixed platform window* (retention is a records-management decision firms make differently and legitimately, and the bounds already prevent the answers that are actually wrong); *an unbounded firm window* (an unbounded feed is an unbounded read, and a pointer that lives forever outlives its access check forever); *sweeping read rows and keeping unread ones* (an unread ninety-day-old notification is not an outstanding task — it is evidence the thing it names is tracked somewhere else — and a feed that only ever grows for the members who ignore it rewards ignoring it); *deleting silently* (a badge of zero is an assertion, and the platform has just destroyed its own evidence for it); *folding the swept count into the unread count* (a number that means neither, attached to rows that cannot be opened); *recording what was swept rather than how much* (reintroduces the removed rows in a table nothing filters); *a separate acknowledge route for the tally* (two gestures for one backlog, and a badge that survives the obvious way to clear it).

**Consequences:** one small per-firm table (`notification_swept_unread`: a member handle, a count, two instants), one setting, one step on the existing daily cron pass over the same reachable-active-firm enumeration every other per-firm sweep uses, with one firm's failure quarantined exactly as the outbox sweep quarantines a sick firm database. The sweep is bounded and resumable, so a firm unswept for a month does not present the nightly pass with an unbounded delete. Extends the Notifications delivery decision's retention position rather than amending it; the firm-tier override it deferred lands here instead, because the column and the sweep were being written anyway and retrofitting a bound into a shipped fixed window is a migration plus a fleet-wide behaviour change.
