summary: "A connected integration's credential lives in its own per-firm `integration_credential` table with its own protected-field declaration, gated on a new `integrations:useConnection` permission, rather than in the `firm_secret` vault whose decrypt is gated on `firm:manageSecrets`. The two are different acts: an administrator opening the firm's vault, and the worker using a credential on behalf of a member whose route has already been authorized. They could not be told apart while they shared a protected column, because a protected column carries exactly one declaration and that declaration's permission IS the decrypt gate — so the shipping routes, which gate on `shipping:manage`, would either have refused the member their own route had just admitted, or have been fixed by granting that member read access to every secret the firm holds. The alternatives were rejected on cost: a second declaration on the same column is forbidden by a fitness test asserting one declaration per (table, column); a caller-named gate would have added a parameter to `readProtected` whose only purpose is to not use the declared permission, inside the module two fitness scans exist to protect; and lowering `firm_secret`'s own gate would have weakened it for every secret it will ever hold. A store may now raise exactly one error, `CredentialAccessForbiddenError`, which the registry turns into a `credential-forbidden` dispatch outcome, so `resolve` keeps its promise never to throw and a refused member gets a 403 naming the grant rather than a 500 or a false report that the firm has no postage account."
---

**A credential the worker uses on a member's behalf is not a secret that member is reading, and the permission that governs it should not pretend otherwise.**

**Context:** The shipping subsystem was built, tested, and unreachable. Five of its eight routes answered 501 because the seam that reads a firm's postage credential returned `null`, and the seam had never been implemented. The obvious reading was that the work was simply unfinished.

It was not. The `firm_secret` store had landed — envelope-encrypted, permission-gated, audited — and everything a read of it needs was already assembled in three other route modules. What blocked the implementation was a permission, and it only becomes visible when you try to write the code. A firm's postage key was to be stored in `firm_secret`, whose value column is declared readable with `firm:manageSecrets`. Every shipping route gates on `shipping:manage`. A member holding the second and not the first — which is the ordinary shape of somebody who mails things for a living — would pass the route's gate and then fail the decrypt.

The collision was latent rather than active, which is why nothing had caught it: `shipping:manage` is seeded only to Owner/Admin, and that role also holds `firm:manageSecrets`. It bites the first time a firm edits a role to let an assistant buy postage, which is the first thing a firm would do.

Neither obvious repair survives contact with the mechanism. Granting the member `firm:manageSecrets` is read access to every secret the firm has sealed, traded for the ability to buy a label. And a narrower declaration on the same column is structurally impossible: `PROTECTED_COLUMNS` admits exactly one declaration per (table, column), asserted by a fitness test, and `readProtected` checks that declaration's permission itself rather than accepting one from its caller.

**Decision:** Separate the two acts by separating their storage. A connected integration's credential lives in `integration_credential`, a per-firm table of its own, with its own protected-field declaration gated on a new `integrations:useConnection`. `firm_secret` keeps its administrator gate, unweakened, for the arbitrary firm-owned secrets it exists to hold.

The new permission is a floor rather than an alternative: holding it decrypts nothing on its own, because a credential is only ever reached through a route that has already enforced its own integration's key. That is what makes it safe to grant beside `shipping:manage` rather than in place of it, and it is seeded to the role that holds every integration's manage key today.

The refusal is a typed outcome, not a throw. `resolve` promises never to throw, and a store enforcing a permission would have broken that promise. So a `CredentialStore` may raise exactly one error — `CredentialAccessForbiddenError` — which the registry catches and returns as a new `credential-forbidden` dispatch outcome. It is deliberately not folded into `not-connected`: the account IS connected and the credential IS there, and reporting otherwise would send the member, and the administrator they escalate to, hunting a connection fault that does not exist. The remedy is a grant, so the outcome says so and the route answers 403 naming the permission.

**Why now rather than later:** nothing had ever been stored in `firm_secret` — its only production writer was the connect route, which answered 501 — so the table was empty in every firm and the separation cost no data migration. That window closes the moment a firm connects an account.

**Consequences:** A firm that grants an integration's manage key to a new role must grant `integrations:useConnection` beside it; the refusal names the permission, but pairing them automatically belongs to the settings surface rather than here. A second credentialed adapter shares this table and this gate, which is correct while the gate means "may use a connected integration" and would need revisiting if a firm ever needs per-category credential access. The disconnect route now clears the credential in the same batch as the connection row, so a revoked key does not outlive the only row that pointed at it.
