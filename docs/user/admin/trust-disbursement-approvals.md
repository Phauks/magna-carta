# Trust money-out approvals: your firm's policy

Money leaving a client's trust ledger — a payment to a court, a vendor, or the client, and equally
your own earned fees taken against a finalized bill — is controlled by two settings: **who has the
authority to release client money**, and **whether large payments take a second person as well**.
How much of that protection your firm wants is your choice.

## What your firm starts with

**A member who has the authority to act on client money can make a payment on their own. A member
who does not raises the payment for somebody who does.**

That is the setting every firm starts on, and for most firms it is the right one: the person you
trust with client money does not need a colleague standing over them, and everybody else's payments
land in front of somebody you do trust.

Nothing about it is hidden. It is recorded as version 1 of your firm's policy history, with a
timestamp, exactly like a change you make yourself — so you can see what you started with, and see
every change since.

## Who has the authority

The **authority to act on client money** is a capability you grant to people. Admin and
Attorney roles carry it out of the box; you can grant it to anyone else, or take it away, so "who
counts as having authority" is exactly who your firm says it is.

A member without it can still do everything else with trust money — record deposits, prepare
payments, assemble an earned-fee application. What they cannot do is be the person who releases the
money.

## Adding a ceiling: when a second person is required

Independently of who holds the authority, you can require a **second person** for larger payments:

- **Above a threshold you set**: a payment at or below the amount can be released by one authorised
  person; anything above it needs two people — the one who proposed it and the one who approves it.
  You can also require that the approver hold the authority as well, so a large payment is seen by
  two people who both have it.
- **Always**: every payment out of a client ledger, whatever the amount, takes two people.
- **Off**: no amount requires a second person. This is what your firm starts on.

One thing to know before choosing a threshold: it applies to each payment on its own. Two payments
each at or below the threshold do not add up to one that requires approval — there is no per-payee
or per-day total — so splitting a payment can stay under the line. Every payment is still
permanently recorded, but if that pattern worries you, choose **Always**.

## How it works day to day

1. A member proposes the payment: the amount, the payee, the purpose, the method.
2. If that member has the authority and no second person is required, the payment is made there and
   then, in one step. The record shows they acted alone and on what footing.
3. Otherwise nothing moves. The proposal waits, and the platform tells the proposer **who can
   approve it** rather than only that somebody must. Pending proposals sit in one queue per trust
   account, oldest first, with earned-fee applications in the same queue and labelled as such, so
   one review covers every way money leaves.
4. An approver approves it, rejects it (with a reason the proposer can answer), or the proposer
   withdraws their own request. Approval is the moment the money actually moves, and everything is
   checked again right then — if the ledger no longer has the funds available, or a bill has been
   paid down in the meantime, the approval is refused rather than posted.

If your firm has only one member who can move trust money at all, that member can complete their own
payments even above a threshold — and the record says so explicitly, so the exception is always
visible.

## Changing the policy

Changing any of this requires the authority capability, and every change is recorded permanently:
who changed it, when, and what it was before. A change applies to **new** proposals only — anything
already awaiting approval is decided under the rules that were in force when it was proposed, so
relaxing the policy never quietly unlocks a payment that was proposed under the stricter one, and
tightening it never strands one.
