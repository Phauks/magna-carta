# Review sets — gathering the documents in a case

_The screens are here: a matter's **Discovery** section lists its review sets and starts one, the
**Discovery** row in the sidebar lists every review set you can open across every case, and opening
one gives you its documents and its loads. Loading a production received from another party still
goes through the same upload path any file takes. Coding documents, reading one on screen, and
searching a set are the next pieces of work, and each says so where it would be._

## What it does

Gathers the documents in a case into a named set, so a firm can say exactly what it looked at, where
each thing came from, and whose records it was.

That sounds administrative. It is not. Almost every question you will be asked about discovery is a
question about that set: what did you search, whose files did you search, how many documents were
there, what could you not read, and how do you know you did not lose any. A review set is the thing
those questions are asked of, and every answer comes out of it as a record rather than as somebody's
recollection.

Three ideas are worth knowing before you use it.

**A review set belongs to one matter, and a matter can have several.** "Our collection", "Plaintiff's
production, March", "Second request" are three different sets with three different stories. Keeping
them apart is what stops a search of your own client's files from quietly returning the other side's
production — which is the difference between a search you can describe and one you cannot.

**Nothing is copied.** When you pull your own matter documents into a review set, no second copy of
anything is made. The set points at the documents you already have, exactly as they are, in the
places they already live. That means your legal holds, your retention rules, and your access rules
all still apply, in one place, with one answer each.

**What you loaded is what you reviewed.** A document is pulled in as it stands on the day you pull
it. If somebody later edits that document and saves a new version, the review set does not change.
That is deliberate: the set has to keep meaning what it meant on the day you certified it. If you
want the newer version in the set, you load it — and that gets its own entry in the record, with your
name and the date on it.

## Making a review set

1. Open the matter, go to **Discovery**, and choose **New** → **New Review Set**.
2. Give it a name you would use in a letter: "Our collection", "Defendant's second production". It is
   what everybody will call this body of material for the rest of the case.
3. It starts empty and open, and **only you can see it** until you add somebody — a review set's list
   is separate from who works the matter. It carries a **document limit** — a large one — and it will
   tell you before you go past it rather than after.

Searching a set is not switched on yet, in either form. The set records whether the semantic half is
wanted, and nothing acts on that answer, so the choice is not put in front of you: turning it on
would start a real, metered cost for a benefit nobody has measured on legal material, and the
decision cannot be unmade once the first document has been prepared. It is left until there is an
answer worth acting on.

## Saying whose records these are

Before or while you load, name the **Custodians of Record** — the people or organizations whose files
and mailboxes the material came from.

Always the full phrase: "Custodian of Record". The firm's safe custody register also has custodians,
and they are a completely different thing (the person a physical original belongs to). You will meet
both in the same matter, so neither is ever shortened to the bare word.

- A Custodian of Record can be somebody already in your directory, or just a name — a production will
  routinely name people your firm has never met.
- If material arrived with nobody attached to it, record that as **unattributed**. It is a real
  answer and a better one than a blank.
- A Custodian of Record belongs to their review set. "J. Smith" in this case and "J. Smith" in
  another case are not assumed to be the same person, because being wrong about that is the kind of
  thing you get asked about under oath.
- If you later work out who "J. Smith" actually is, correct the Custodian of Record rather than
  making a second one. Everything already attributed to them follows the correction.

## Loading material

Loading is always a **declared act**: you say what is coming before it comes. That is what makes it
possible, afterwards, to say "we expected 4,000 documents and 3,987 arrived" instead of only "3,987
documents are here".

A load says four things.

**Where it came from.** One of:

- **Your own matter files** — documents already in this matter's file area.
- **Your own filed email** — email already filed to this matter. The message and its attachments come
  in together and stay together.
- **A production received from another party** — new files arriving from outside, which take the
  same upload and virus-scanning path every other file in the system takes. Nothing about discovery
  skips that.
- **Collected from a client's own system** — named, and deliberately not built. If you ask for it you
  will be told so plainly, rather than finding an option missing and wondering. Collect to a drive
  and load it as a received production.

**How much is coming.** Your best count. It is never used to correct what actually arrives — it is
there so the two can be compared.

**Whose records these are.** Either one Custodian of Record for the whole load, or per document when
a production carries several people's material.

**Whether the firm's own assistant transcripts are in scope.** This one is required and has no
default, and it is worth explaining why.

Conversations your firm has with the in-app assistant are **your work product** — your own thinking
about the case, not the client's records. They are kept, they are deletable, and they are
discoverable in much the same way email is. But whether your own reasoning about a matter belongs in
a set you are about to certify the contents of is a decision for a person, not a setting.

So every load asks, and you answer. There is no default in either direction on purpose: a box quietly
ticked "no" decides it for you just as much as one quietly ticked "yes" — it just fails the other
way, and you would only find out after you produced. Your answer is kept with the load, so the record
can say what the set was drawn from and not only what ended up in it.

Right now, choosing to include transcripts will tell you that the part which reaches them is not
built yet. That is a straight answer rather than a silently ignored request: if the system had
accepted it and done nothing, your record would claim a search you did not run.

## What happens when you load your own files

1. Declare the load.
2. Choose the documents, or the filed email.
3. The set fills. No files are copied, nothing is uploaded, and nothing is re-scanned — the system
   already knows what these documents are and that they are clean.

Two things will stop a load, both by name rather than by silently skipping:

- **A file that is quarantined or has not finished being scanned.** A file the system is not willing
  to open is not review material, and quietly leaving it out of a set of fifty would give you
  forty-eight documents and no idea which two were missing.
- **A file from another matter.** A review set is one case's set.

If you run the same load twice — a lost connection, a retried click — you get the same documents
once, not twice.

## Locking and closing

A review set moves one way: **open**, then **locked**, then **closed**.

- **Locked** stops new material going in. Everything still reads normally.
- **Closed** additionally releases the set's working files — the extracted text and the search index,
  both of which can be rebuilt from your original documents at any time. Every record survives:
  every document row, every load, and every report.

There is no reopening a closed set. That is deliberate rather than restrictive: a set you could
reopen is a set whose contents depend on when somebody looked at it, which is precisely what a review
set exists to prevent. If you need to look at more material, make a new set and load into it — which
leaves a record of when you did and who decided to.

## Tips and edge cases

- **Split large productions across loads rather than fighting the limit.** Each load is its own
  record with its own count, which is a better story than one enormous load whose edges nobody can
  describe.
- **Loading is not the same as having read something.** A set can hold documents whose text has not
  been read out yet; that comes next and reports itself honestly, including everything it could not
  read.
- **A virus-flagged file in a case is evidence.** It is quarantined and kept, listed in the set, and
  never opened — not deleted. Deleting evidence because it is unpleasant is not a thing this system
  does.
- **Access to a case does not give access to its review sets.** They are granted separately, because
  material another party produced may be restricted to attorneys by court order. If you can see the
  matter and not the set, that is the system working — and a set you are not on does not appear at
  all rather than appearing and refusing you.
- **`/discovery` in the sidebar is the screen to live on if reviewing is most of your work.** It
  lists every review set you can open across every case, with the case named on the row. A case you
  have no access to shows as unnamed, which is the same rule running the other way.
- **A large selection of files takes several passes.** The platform adds documents in batches; if it
  stops partway, opening the same load again continues from where it got to rather than adding
  anything twice.
