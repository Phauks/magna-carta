# Attorney License Lookup

Every state publishes its own register of who is licensed to practise law there, and that register
is the authority on whether a lawyer is in good standing. Attorney License Lookup takes you to the
right one. Find it under **Tools → Attorney License Lookup**.

It covers all fifty states and the District of Columbia.

## Looking a lawyer up

1. Choose the state the lawyer is admitted in. Type the state's name or its two-letter code.
2. The register for that state appears, named after the authority that keeps it.
3. Choose **Open the Register**. It opens in a new tab, and you search there.

Bar admission is per state, so the state you want is the one the lawyer is admitted in, which is not
always the state they work in or the state your matter is in. A lawyer admitted in several states
appears in several registers, and standing in one says nothing about standing in another.

## What you are looking at

The authority differs by state, and the register is named for it. In most states it is the
mandatory bar association's member directory. In states without a unified bar it is the judiciary,
supreme court or disciplinary board's own attorney-registration search — New York's Office of Court
Administration, the Ohio Supreme Court, the Illinois ARDC and others.

New Hampshire and South Dakota publish no instant public name search. There the link takes you to
the official verification entry point instead, where verification is by request.

## Magna Carta does not look them up for you

Magna Carta sends no query to the register, reads nothing back, and keeps no record of what you
found.

That is deliberate rather than a limitation. A lawyer's standing is exactly the kind of fact that
changes after you look: a suspension recorded today makes yesterday's answer wrong. A copy of a
register's answer, held here and quietly going stale, would be worse than no answer at all, because
it would look like an answer. So you always read the register first-hand and current.

For the same reason, nothing here is attached to a matter and nothing is billed.

## When a register is not encrypted

Two states publish their register over an unencrypted connection. The screen says so before you go.

It matters because of what you are about to type. On such a page, the name of the lawyer you are
asking about and the answer that comes back can both be read by anyone able to observe the network
between you and the register — on shared or public wi-fi, that is a real possibility. If the fact
that you are checking a particular lawyer is itself sensitive, use a connection you trust.

The register is still the authority whatever it is served over, so the link is still offered.
