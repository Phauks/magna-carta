# Asking a question of a matter's documents

If your firm has turned it on, you can ask a matter's documents an ordinary question — the way you
would ask a colleague who had read them — instead of guessing which words the document happens to use.

_"Where did we agree to a contingency on appeal?"_

What comes back is **passages from your own documents**, each with the document, the version, and the
page it came from. It is a way of finding the paragraph, not a way of being told the answer.

## It returns passages, not answers

This is the thing worth understanding before you rely on it, so it is first.

Nothing writes you a summary, a conclusion, or a recommendation. You get the actual sentences out of
the actual documents, and you read them and decide what they mean. No paragraph appears that a lawyer
at your firm did not write.

That is deliberate rather than a limitation waiting to be lifted. A composed answer would be a
paragraph you cannot check without going and reading the documents anyway — and the one thing you
could not check is what it left out.

## How it differs from ordinary search

Ordinary search finds the words you typed. This finds passages that **mean** something close to what
you asked, so it will find a clause about "fees on appellate review" when you asked about
"contingency on appeal".

The trade goes both ways, and the honest version is:

- **Use a question** when you know what you are looking for but not what it is called — the concepts
  a matter's documents discuss under half a dozen different names.
- **Use ordinary search** when you know the exact term, the case number, the defined term, or the
  name. Exact-match searching is better at exact matches, and it always will be.

Neither replaces the other. Nothing about this changes the search you already use.

## What it can see

**The committed documents on the matter you are looking at, and nothing else.**

- Only that matter. A question you ask on one matter does not reach another, and the results are
  re-checked against what you personally are allowed to see before any of them reaches your screen.
- Only saved versions. A document somebody has open and has not saved yet is not there.
- **Never material received from an opposing party.** Discovery review sets are a separate corpus and
  are never searched here — an opponent's production does not turn up in a search of your own files.

Your firm can also leave a specific matter out entirely. If a matter has been left out, this search
finds nothing on it — not "a few things", nothing.

## The freshness line, and why it is there

Every set of results carries a sentence like:

> Current as of 14:05. 3 documents added since then are not yet searchable.

Take it literally. The index updates on a schedule — roughly hourly — so a document filed a few
minutes ago is genuinely not findable here yet. Filing something and immediately searching for it
will not work, and no setting makes it work.

The line appears **even when the number is zero**, so that "nothing pending" is something you were
told rather than something you have to infer from the absence of a warning. When documents are
pending, the search may ask the index to catch up, and will say that it is doing so.

If you need certainty that you have seen everything filed today, go to the matter's documents rather
than relying on this.

## An empty result means nothing matched

If nothing in the matter's documents is close enough to your question, you get an empty result, and
it says so plainly.

**It does not mean the matter is thin, and nothing is invented to fill the gap.** It means those
words, on that matter, matched nothing above the threshold. Reasonable next steps:

- Ask it differently. A question phrased around what the document would say, rather than what you
  want to know, tends to do better: _"fee to be paid from the recovery"_ rather than _"do we get paid
  if we win"_.
- Check the freshness line — the thing you are after may be in the documents that are not searchable
  yet.
- Fall back to ordinary search on the exact term.
- Check with your administrator whether this matter has been left out.

## Tips

- **One clear question beats a pile of keywords.** The feature is matching meaning, so a fragment of
  a sentence gives it more to work with than three nouns.
- **Longer documents are read in pieces.** A passage comes back with its page, so a hit deep inside a
  four-hundred-page brief points you at the page rather than at the file.
- **Citations are current.** A document renamed since the index last updated is cited by its current
  name, and a document deleted since then produces no result at all rather than a link into nothing.
- **Your question is not stored anywhere.** It is not written into the matter's history, into any log,
  or into any record your firm can read back. What is recorded is that you searched this matter and
  how many results came back.
