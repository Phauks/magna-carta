# Meetings

_You can start a video meeting from any calendar event, join one other people have started, see and
hear everybody in the room, share your screen, and read back afterwards who was there and when. It is
part of Magna Carta, so your firm needs no separate conferencing account and the record of the meeting
sits on the event it belongs to. People at your firm join from the event; a client, opposing counsel or
anybody else on the event can be sent a link that brings them to the meeting's door, where a host lets
them in. A meeting on a matter can be recorded, with everybody in it told, and a recording on the matter
gets a written transcript that names who said what, and a summary with its action items. There is no
telephone number to dial in on._

## What it does

Turns a calendar event into a place you can actually meet. A hearing preparation, a partners' meeting,
a case review: whatever the event is, the people already on it can be in the same room without anybody
sending a link, and the case file remembers that the meeting happened.

Three things are worth knowing before you use it.

**A meeting lives on an event; it is not a separate thing to arrange.** You do not create a meeting,
invite people to it and give it a time. The event already has a time and already says who is on it, so
the meeting simply happens there. That also means there is only ever one meeting per event — everybody
who presses the button on the same event ends up in the same room.

**Who may join is who may see the event.** A meeting on a matter's event can be joined by the people
who can see that matter; a firm event's meeting is open to the firm. Nobody gets into a room by
knowing an address, and nothing about a meeting reveals a matter you would not otherwise see. Somebody
from outside the firm comes in only on a link a host sent them, and only as far as the door until a
host lets them in — see **Inviting a guest** below.

**Who was there is kept; what was said is kept only if somebody records it.** Unless a meeting is
recorded, nothing said out loud is kept, and nothing on video ever is. What the record always keeps is
attendance: each person who joined, when they joined and when they left, plus the meeting's chat. That
is the part a file needs six months later, and it is the part that lives beside the documents and the
correspondence rather than in somebody's memory. Recording the sound of a meeting is a separate,
deliberate act, and everybody in the room is told when it happens; see **Recording a meeting** below.

## Starting a meeting

1. Open the event — from the firm calendar, from a matter's **Calendar** section, or from the matter
   timeline. Any of them opens the same entry window.
2. Press **Start meeting**. The meeting opens on its own screen and you are in the room.
3. Your browser asks to use your microphone and camera. Say yes, and everybody who joins after you can
   see and hear you. You can turn either off once you are in.

The first person to press the button is the one who starts the meeting, and they are the host of it
until they hand it to somebody else; everybody else gets **Join meeting** in the same place from then
on. Opening a room from an event needs the permission to host
meetings, so a member who may join meetings but not host them sees **Join meeting** once somebody
else has started one and nothing before that.

If **Start meeting** is not on the event at all, your firm has not been set up for meetings yet, and
that is a step for whoever administers the firm rather than something you can turn on from here.

## Audio calls

Beside **Start meeting** is **Start call**, and it opens the same kind of room with the cameras left
out of it. Use it when the point is the conversation: a quick word before a hearing, a client
catch-up, anything you would have picked up the telephone for.

What is different, and nothing else is:

- **Your camera is never asked for.** The browser asks for your microphone alone, so no indicator
  light comes on and there is nothing to remember to turn off.
- **Everybody is a name, not a picture.** Each person in the room is their initials and their name,
  with the same mark beside them when they are muted.
- **There is no screen sharing**, because there is no picture in the room to share one into.

**A call cannot be turned into a video meeting once it has started.** Everybody who joined agreed to
a call, so nobody can add a camera to the room afterwards. If it turns out you need to see each
other, leave and start a meeting on the same event — the call's attendance stays on the record, and
the meeting's is recorded beside it.

Joining is the same act: where somebody has started a call, the event says **Join call** rather than
**Join meeting**, and the Meetings page marks the call rows with a telephone.

## Joining a meeting

Open the same event. Where **Start meeting** was, it now says **Join meeting**, with a count of how
many people are already in the room. Press it and you are in.

A meeting holds three hundred people. The three hundred and first is told the room is full rather
than being let in halfway. Rooms of more than a dozen read differently once you are in them, and
**Large meetings** below is what changes.

## The Meetings page

**Meetings**, under Communications in the sidebar, is the other way in — for when you know there is a
meeting and would rather not go looking for the event it belongs to. It has three parts:

- **Live now** — the meetings running at this moment, with how many people are in each room. **Join**
  takes you straight in; there is no second step.
- **Upcoming** — the meetings already opened on events today or later, with the day and time of the
  event they belong to, in that event's own time zone. **Join** works here too, so you can be first
  into a room rather than waiting for somebody else to arrive.
- **History** — what finished in the last thirty days, with when it ended, where it was filed (the
  matter's name, or Firm event), what became of its recording (Not recorded, Being prepared, Saved,
  Failed or Interrupted), **Attendance** for who was in the room, **Recordings** where the meeting was
  recorded, and a way back to the event.

Each row names the event and says whether it belongs to a firm event or to a matter; where it is a
matter, the row names the matter and links back to it.

## A meeting's address

Every meeting has its own address ending in nine digits, such as magnacarta.esq/meeting/482-917-305.
That is what the address bar shows once you are in the room, and what **Copy Link** in the room
copies. The nine digits are easy to read out on a call; anybody at the firm who may join the meeting
can type the address with or without the dashes. Links in the older form, ending in the meeting's
long reference, still work and show the nine-digit address once the room opens.

## Your own room

If you can host meetings, the Meetings page shows a **My Room** card. Your room is a link that stays
the same, so a colleague can keep it and use it whenever you are in, without anybody putting a
meeting on the calendar first.

- **Set Up My Room** makes your room the first time. Nothing is made until you press it.
- **Copy Link** copies your room's address to send to a colleague. The address is your first name,
  such as magnacarta.esq/room/trevor. Where somebody at the firm already has that name, it is your
  first and last names, and then a number.
- **Open My Room** starts a meeting in your room and takes you into it. The meeting is put on your
  calendar as an event in your name, starting now. If your room is already open, the button reads
  **Go to My Room** and takes you back in.

The room's settings are in the **...** menu beside the card's title:

- **Hold arrivals in a lobby** is on to begin with, so you let each person in yourself. Turn it off
  to let colleagues straight in.
- **Open as an audio call** starts your room's meetings without video.

A colleague who follows your link before you have opened the room is told you are not in yet. The
page checks again every few seconds, and **Join** appears as soon as you open it. Joining puts the
meeting on their calendar too.

When everybody has left, the meeting ends by itself the next time you open your room, and its
calendar entry is closed at the time the last person left.

Your room's address does not change, even if your name does.

### Everybody's rooms

The **Firm Rooms** card lists every member of the firm and their room, whether or not anybody is in
it. Each row says **In a meeting** with how many people are there, **Not in a meeting**, or **No room
set up**. **Copy Link** copies a colleague's room address so you can send it ahead of time, and
**Join** appears while their room is open. The list checks again every thirty seconds. It does not
say what a meeting is about.

### Moving a meeting onto a matter

A meeting in your room starts on your own calendar because, when it opens, nobody has said what it
is about. Once they have, press **Move to a Matter** in the meeting's header, during the meeting or
after it, and choose the matter. The calendar entry moves onto the matter with everybody on it, the
meeting keeps its address so nobody in the room is disturbed, and from then on it can be recorded
like any other meeting on a matter. A meeting cannot be moved back, or moved a second time.

The room's owner, or anybody hosting that meeting, can move it.

## While you are in the meeting

Everybody in the room has a tile of their own, with your own picture pinned so you can see what you
are showing. A gold edge around a tile means that person is talking. Down the side is the list of who
is in the room, with the same marks beside each name: who is speaking, who is muted, who is sharing a
screen. Along the bottom are the controls:

- **Microphone** — mutes and unmutes you. Muting stops your sound reaching the room; nothing is
  recorded either way.
- **Camera** — turns your picture on and off. With it off your tile stays in the room under your name,
  so people can still see you are there.
- **Share screen** — shows the others what is on your screen. Your browser asks which window or which
  whole screen to share, and everybody in the room sees it beside the faces rather than instead of
  them. Press it again to stop sharing. Your camera keeps running while you share, so you are not
  reduced to a disembodied document.
- **Speaker view** — shows whoever is talking large, with the last few speakers in a strip beside
  them, instead of an even grid. It changes how the room looks to you and to nobody else.
- **Leave** — takes you out of the room. The meeting carries on without you and the record notes the
  time you left. Pressing **Join meeting** again puts you back in, and the record shows both stretches.

If you close the tab, or your connection drops, you are shown as having left within about a minute.
So you cannot get stuck in a room by walking away from it, and nobody is left looking at a tile with
nobody behind it for long.

### Changing the name you go by

To go by another name in a meeting, such as the conference room you are sitting in, press the pencil
on your own row under **People** and type the name. It is for this meeting only. Everybody else sees
the name you joined with beside it ("Joined as ..."), so a chosen name never hides who you are, and
the meeting's attendance record keeps your account's name. Your chosen name stays if your connection
drops and comes back. **Use My Account Name** in the same window puts your own name back in one
press. A guest can change their name the same way, and goes back with **Use the Name I Joined With**.

## Connection quality

Beside each person's name, on their tile and in the list down the side, is a small set of bars
saying how well their picture and sound are reaching you. Four bars is good, fewer is worse, and an
empty mark means nothing has been measured yet, which is how every meeting looks for its first few
seconds. Your own preview carries the mark too, and that one is about what you are SENDING: when
everybody tells you that you are breaking up, this is where you find out whether they are right.

**Connection** in the control bar opens the numbers behind the marks:

- **Quality**, with a line saying what it means. Good is under 2% of packets lost and a round trip
  under 200 ms. Fair (the picture may soften) is 2 to 8% lost or 200 to 400 ms. Poor (expect
  freezes and broken sound) is more than that.
- **Server**: the Cloudflare site your meeting runs through, by its code (named for the nearest
  airport, such as SJC), whether your media goes straight to it or through a relay, and its
  address.
- **Picture**: the size of the picture you are sending, in pixels.
- **Camera Size**: your camera is sent at full, half and quarter size, and each person receives
  the size their screen shows you at. This row names the size whose picture is shown above, with
  its own rate.
- **Upload** and **Download**: everything your connection is sending and receiving, all streams
  together.
- **Packets Lost** and **Round Trip**: how much of what you send goes missing, and how long a
  message takes to reach the server and come back.

When other people are in the room, the same mark for each of them is listed beside your own
numbers.

**If the picture flashes,** press **Copy Diagnostics** straight after it happens and send what it
copies to whoever is helping you. The times in it line up with the flash, which says whether the
picture was restarted or the browser simply drew a frame wrong.

**None of it is recorded.** The measurements live in this browser tab while the meeting is open and
go when you close it. Nothing about your connection reaches the meeting's own record, which stays
what it has always been: who was in the room and for how long.

A poor mark on one person and good marks on everybody else usually means the trouble is at their
end. Poor marks on everybody, or on your own preview, usually means it is at yours.

## Chat

**Chat** in the control bar opens what is being said in writing, beside what is being said out loud.
A number on the control counts what has been said since you last looked, so you can watch the room
instead of watching a button. Opening the panel clears it.

**Messages arrive as they are said.** The room passes each one along the moment it reaches the
record, so a colleague's message is on your screen while they are still looking at the send button,
and a message taken back goes blank on every screen at once. The panel still reads the record behind
that, quietly and less often, which is what catches you up on anything that did not reach you — a
connection that dropped and came back, a tab that was asleep.

Each line says who said it and when. Type in the box at the foot and press Enter to send it; Shift
and Enter writes a second line instead. One message holds 4,000 characters, and the panel starts
telling you how much room is left as you approach that. You have to be in the meeting to say
anything in it: once you have left, the panel is a record you can read and not a place you can
write.

**What is said here is kept on the meeting's record.** Chat is the one part of a meeting that is
written down anywhere — the pictures and the sound are carried between browsers and never reach the
platform at all — so a message in a meeting belongs on the file the way an email does. Write it
accordingly.

**Taking a message back leaves a mark.** Your own messages carry **Take back**, and so does every
message for whoever is running the meeting. The words go and the line stays, saying that a message
was taken back. Nothing is deleted outright, because the record is an account of what happened, and
that something was said and then removed is part of what happened.

## Raising your hand and reacting

Two ways to say something without interrupting anybody, and neither is kept: a raised hand and a
reaction live for as long as the meeting does and leave nothing on the record. What was said in
chat is on the file; that you agreed with a sentence four seconds ago is not.

**Raise hand** is the hand in the control bar. Pressing it puts a mark beside your name — on your
tile, on your row in the list down the side, and in the queue whoever is running the meeting works
through. Pressing it again takes it down, and so does a host clearing the floor. It is a request
for the floor rather than a setting: nothing about your microphone changes, and nobody is muted or
unmuted by it.

**The queue is in the order the hands went up**, which is why it is worth using in a room of thirty
rather than catching somebody's eye. A host opens it from **Hands** in the control bar, which
carries the number of people waiting so nobody is left with their hand up while everybody watches
the room instead of a button.

**Reactions are six, and that is all of them**: Agree, Disagree, Thank you, That is funny, Well
said, and Congratulations. Press the face in the control bar and pick one; it rises off your tile,
everybody sees it for about four seconds, and it is gone. There is no seventh, nothing to type, and
no emoji anywhere in this product — the six are drawn with the same icons as the rest of the
screen.

**One at a time.** A hand going up or down and a reaction each take a moment before the next one
will send, so a room of three hundred applauding is a room the connection can carry. Pressing again
inside that moment does nothing rather than queueing.

**In a large meeting the reactions become a count.** Above sixteen people the room stops saying who
reacted and says how many did, as a short row beside the meeting's own details at the top of the
screen. That is the honest answer at that size: only sixteen tiles are on your screen, so a mark on
the right face is a promise the screen could not keep for most of the room. A raised hand is still
named, however big the room, because it is a request somebody has to answer.

**If you use a screen reader**, a hand going up is announced whatever the size of the room, and a
reaction is announced where the room named who threw it. The count row is not read out: a stream of
changing numbers once a second is noise rather than information, and the reactions control says
what it offers.

**If you have asked for less motion**, the reaction appears on the tile and fades where it is
instead of rising. It stays for the same four seconds, because how long it is there is part of what
it says.

## Polls and questions

Two more ways for a room to talk to whoever is running it, and unlike a hand or a reaction, **both
are kept on the meeting's record**, the way chat is. A poll is a host asking the room a question with
set answers; a question is the room asking the host.

**Polls** in the control bar opens the polls. A number on the control counts the open polls waiting
for your answer, and when one opens while the panel is shut you are told once, in a short note at
the corner of the screen, so a poll does not sit unanswered because nobody was looking at a button.
The polls and the questions keep themselves up to date while you are in the room; opening a panel
also checks for anything new.

Each poll is a card. Where you may choose one answer the options are a set of round buttons; where
you may choose several they are tick boxes, and the card says how many you may pick. **Answer**
sends your choice and stays unavailable until what you have picked is something the poll will take.
A **ranking** lists every option with arrows to move each one up or down; put them in order, best at
the top, and press **Answer**. A **score** asks for one number on its scale, such as 1 to 5.
**An answer cannot be changed**: once it is sent, the card turns into the result, one bar per option
with how many chose it, and a line such as "12 of 30 answered". A ranking's result lists the options
in the order the room put them, each with its average place; a score's result gives the average,
such as "Average 3.8 out of 5", above how many gave each number. A poll a host has closed shows its
result to everybody.

**"Answers are not shown with names"** at the top of a poll means exactly that: nothing in the
product, including what the host sees, says who chose what. It does not hide that you took part.
The card says it before you answer, so you know what you are answering into.

**Asking the room (hosts).** Whoever is running the meeting sees **New Poll** at the top of the same
panel: the question, the **Poll Type**, between two and ten answers (add and remove them as you go),
and whether to show names with answers. The poll types are **One** (each person picks one answer),
**Multiple** (each person picks up to the number you choose), **Ranking** (each person puts every
answer in order) and **Score** (each person gives a number on a scale of 1 to 3, 1 to 5 or 1 to 10,
so you do not type answers). **Open Poll** puts it in front of the
room at once, and your card shows the result, moving as people answer. A poll another host opened is
a question to you as much as to anybody: you answer it first, the number on **Polls** counts it, and
you see its result once you have answered. Several polls can be open together. **Close
Poll** asks first, because a closed poll takes no more answers and cannot be opened again. On a poll
that shows names, **See Who Answered** lists each person and what they chose.

**Questions are part of chat.** To ask one, tick **Ask as a question** under the chat box and press
**Ask**; a question holds 1,000 characters and the box counts down what is left. It appears in the
chat with a question mark beside it, among the messages, in the order things were said. **Questions**
at the top of the chat shows only the questions, sorted as below, and **All** brings the messages
back. The number beside **Chat** on the side panel, with a question mark, is how many questions are
still waiting for an answer.

**The Questions view sorts itself.** Questions nobody has answered come first, and among those, the ones with
the most upvotes. Press the arrow beside a question to add your upvote, and press it again to take
your upvote back; the order moves as the room votes, without you having to reopen anything.

**Working the queue (hosts).** A host sees **Mark Answered** on each question, and **Mark
Unanswered** to take the mark back if the wrong one was marked. **Redact** blanks a question and
leaves a line where it was, the same way taking back a chat message does; a host can redact any
question, and you can redact your own.

## Sound and picture treatment

**Effects** in the control bar, beside the camera, shows what your browser is doing to your sound and
your picture before they reach anybody else. Nothing about it leaves your computer or reaches the
meeting's record, and the room sees and hears only the result.

**Reduce background noise** asks your browser to clean up your microphone: it takes out steady noise
such as a fan or a road, and alongside it the browser cancels echo and evens out your volume. It is
on unless you turn it off. The line under the switch says what your browser reports it is doing;
some browsers apply these without saying so, and then the line says your browser decides this
itself. The switch shows what the browser actually did, so if it could not do what you asked, the
switch stays where it was and the panel says so.

**Stronger noise removal** is a second switch beneath it, off unless you turn it on. It runs a noise
filter on your own computer instead of asking the browser, and takes out fans, typing and street
noise more thoroughly than the browser does. While it is on, the browser's own noise reduction steps
aside so the two do not both work on your voice, and the panel says "Background noise is being
removed on this computer before anyone hears you." It uses a little more of your computer's power
and delays your sound by a few hundredths of a second. If it stops working partway through a
meeting, the switch turns itself off, your browser's noise reduction takes over again, and the panel
tells you; you are never left unheard because the filter failed. Where your browser cannot run it,
the switch is not shown.

**Your background** can be left as it is, blurred, or replaced with a picture. Open **Effects** and
choose **None**, **Blur** or **Image**. The first time you choose **Image**, the panel asks you to pick
a picture from your computer (PNG, JPEG or WebP); after that, **Image** uses the same picture until
you pick a different one. The picture is kept in your browser and is never uploaded: the people in
the meeting see you in front of it, and nobody receives the picture itself.

Blurring and replacing are done by your own computer while you are in the meeting, so a slower
computer may send a slightly lower frame rate with an effect on. If the effect stops working, your
camera carries on as it is and the panel tells you why; you are never left with no picture because an
effect failed. On a browser too old to draw the effect, the panel offers whatever the browser itself
offers instead, or says "Background blur is not available in this browser." In a call there is no
picture, so there is no background to choose. Turning **Reduce background noise** on or off never
changes your background: only pressing **None**, **Blur** or **Image** does that.

**Your choice is remembered on this computer**, and applied every time you join a meeting from it.
It is kept by this browser rather than on your account, because what a computer can do is a fact
about that computer: a laptop that cannot blur should not be told you always blur. The picture you
chose for **Image** is remembered the same way.

## Large meetings

A room holds up to three hundred people, and a meeting that size is read differently from a meeting
of four. Nothing below has to be turned on; it is what the screen does once the room is big enough to
need it.

**You see a page of the room at a time.** Up to sixteen tiles, with a count and **Page 2 of 19** under
them, and arrows either side to walk through the rest. The order is not the order people arrived:
anybody you have pinned comes first, then whoever is sharing a screen, then the people who have been
talking, then everybody else. So page one is the part of the room the meeting is actually happening
in, and somebody who starts talking on page twelve comes to you rather than waiting to be found.

**Only what you are looking at is sent to you.** Your browser asks for the pictures of the people on
your page and the sound of the people talking, and nothing else. That is what makes a room of three
hundred work on an ordinary connection, and it is why most people in a large meeting are a name and
their initials rather than a picture: their tile says **Not shown**, which means they are in the room
and their camera is simply not being sent to you at the moment. It is not a picture that failed to
arrive, and turning the page changes which pictures you get.

**You hear more of the room than you see.** Sound and pictures are chosen separately, so somebody
answering you from page four is heard perfectly well while their tile is nowhere on your screen.

**Pin** keeps somebody on your screen whatever else the room does. It is on every tile, it moves them
to the front of the first page, and it is yours alone: pinning a colleague changes nothing about what
anybody else is looking at. Press it again to let them go.

**The list beside the room gains a search** once there are more than a couple of dozen people in it.
It narrows the list as you type and answers the question a long list cannot: whether somebody has
arrived yet.

## Running a meeting as a host

Somebody runs every meeting, and in most meetings nobody has to think about it: whoever pressed
**Start meeting** is the host, and that is the end of it. In a room of thirty it matters. A line is
carrying a building site, somebody is in the room who should not be, everybody has arrived and the
door should be shut, one person is presenting and should be on every screen. The controls below are
for that, and only the people running the meeting see them.

**Being the host is about this meeting, not about your seat at the firm.** Your firm decides who may
open a meeting at all; who is running THIS one is a separate thing, written on the meeting itself,
and it changes during the meeting as you hand it over or name somebody to help. So a partner who may
start meetings has no say over a meeting they are not hosting, and a paralegal handed a room runs it
for as long as they hold it. The badges on the tiles and down the side of the room say who that is,
and everybody can see them — you should not have to open a menu to find out who to ask for the floor.

**Co-hosts share the work.** **Host controls** on somebody's tile or their row offers **Make
co-host**, and a co-host can do everything below except decide who runs the room: naming another
co-host, withdrawing one and handing the meeting over stay with the host, and a co-host cannot
remove the host or mute them with **Mute everyone**. Withdraw a co-host from the same menu, or from
the list of hosts. It lasts for this meeting and says nothing about any other.

**Handing the meeting over** is **Hand hosting over** on the person's menu. They run it from then on
and you become a co-host. It asks first, because taking it back needs them to hand it to you.

**A room nobody is running can be claimed.** Hosting does not pass on its own when a host leaves — a
host whose connection drops for a minute has not stopped hosting, and a meeting that reassigned
itself would hand your client call to whoever happened to be next in the list. So when the host has
gone and nobody else is running the room, anybody at the firm who may start meetings can open **Host
controls › Hosts** and press **Claim hosting**. The record says the room was claimed rather than
handed over, because nobody gave it. While somebody IS running the meeting the control is not
offered: a room already has a host.

### Quieting the room

**Mute** on somebody's menu turns their microphone off. It is a real mute and not a request: their
sound stops reaching the room whatever their browser does, and their screen says a host muted them.
They can turn their own microphone back on whenever they like — muting somebody is how you deal with
a noisy line, not a way to keep them quiet.

**Mute everyone** in **Host controls** does the same for the whole room at once, leaving you and any
co-hosts able to speak. It is what you press before you start talking, not a setting: anybody can
unmute themselves straight afterwards.

**Ask to unmute** is the opposite, and it is deliberately not the obvious thing. Nothing in Magna
Carta can open somebody's microphone from your click. The person you ask sees a line saying you
asked, with **Unmute** and **Stay muted** beside it, and nothing at all happens until they choose.
They may be sitting with a client, on a call they muted themselves for precisely so that the next
two minutes were not in the meeting, and a microphone opened for them would put that conversation
into the room with nothing in front of them to say it had happened. You will see no acknowledgement
either way, because there is nothing to acknowledge: you asked.

### Putting one person on every screen

**Spotlight for everyone** puts one person in the large slot on every screen in the room, ahead of
anything anybody has pinned, and asks for their picture at full size however many tiles are on the
page. Use it for whoever is presenting. It is the difference between a spotlight and a pin: pinning
arranges your own screen and says nothing to anybody, spotlighting decides what the room is looking
at. A spotlit tile carries a mark so nobody has to guess why somebody jumped to the front.

**Stop spotlighting** clears it, from the same menu or from **Host controls**. It clears itself when
the person leaves, and it is gone when the meeting ends.

### Who is in the room, and who is not

**Remove from the meeting** takes somebody out of the room. It asks first, because it is not a step
you can take back: their screen says a host removed them, they cannot rejoin this meeting, and the
record keeps when their stay began and when it ended. It is this MEETING they are out of and nothing
else — a meeting on a new calendar entry is a new room, and their place on this entry is
untouched. A co-host cannot remove the host, and nobody removes themselves this way: **Leave** is how
a person leaves.

**Lock the room** shuts the door. Nobody new can come in, everybody already in the room stays exactly
where they are, and the event and the Meetings page both say **Locked** instead of offering a way in
— so a colleague arriving late learns it from the entry rather than from being turned away after
pressing Join. **Unlock the room** opens it again. Locking is what you do once everybody has
arrived and the matter turns confidential.

## The waiting room

Most meetings need no door. Everybody who can join is already on the event, and a queue in front of
an ordinary hearing would only make a colleague press a button and then wait for somebody to notice
them. So the waiting room is off unless you turn it on, and it is worth turning on for the meetings
where who walks in matters: a client call, a discussion that turns confidential, a room you would
rather not have somebody wander into halfway through.

**Turning it on.** Beside **Start meeting** and **Start call** on the event is a switch reading
**Hold arrivals in a lobby**. Set it before you open the room and everybody who joins after you waits
until you let them in. The same switch is on the event form itself, under **Where**, once you have
chosen a meeting — so a room you are scheduling for next Tuesday can be set to hold arrivals now
rather than being remembered on the day. Inside a meeting it is **Host controls › Hold arrivals in a
lobby**, and the same item reads **Let arrivals straight in** once it is on, so you can shut the door
in the middle of a meeting that has taken a turn, or open it again once everybody has arrived.

**Turning it off does not let in the people already outside.** They stay at the door until you decide
about each of them. A switch is a standing decision about arrivals in general, and somebody may be
waiting precisely because you meant to turn them away.

**Whoever is running the meeting never waits.** A host or co-host of the meeting goes straight in.
Holding the firm permission to start meetings is not enough on its own: a colleague with that
permission walking past the door of a confidential matter discussion is what the lobby is for.

**A locked room has no queue.** Locking refuses arrivals outright rather than holding them, because a
queue in front of a door somebody has shut is a queue nobody will ever be let in from.

### What somebody waiting sees

The meeting's own screen, saying they are waiting to be let in, which meeting it is, and — where
anybody is in front of them — how many people are ahead. There is no room on it: no tiles, no list of
who is inside, no microphone and no camera. Nothing is captured while they wait, so no indicator
light comes on and there is nothing of theirs going anywhere. The only control is **Leave**, which
closes their own stay and takes them back to the event.

### Letting people in, and turning them away

The control bar carries **Lobby** for whoever is running the meeting, with the number of people
outside on it, and the first time somebody arrives a line says so — you should not have to watch a
button to find out that a client is standing at the door.

Opening it lists everybody waiting, with the time each of them arrived, and **Admit** and **Deny**
beside each name. Past one person there is **Admit all**, which lets in exactly the people the list
was showing: somebody who arrives a second later is still a person you have not decided about, and
they stay at the door. If the room is full, the people who did not get a seat stay in the list and
the panel says how many, so you can end somebody else's stay or wait for a seat.

**A denial bars that person from this meeting.** They are told a host did not let them in, and
pressing Join again will not put them back in the queue — otherwise Deny would be a button that does
nothing at all. It is this meeting they are barred from and nothing else: a meeting on a new calendar
entry is a new room, their place on this entry is untouched, and the record keeps when they
arrived and when their stay closed.

## Inviting a guest

A client call, a settlement conference, a hearing with an expert: most of the meetings worth having
include somebody who is not at your firm. You can send any of them a link to the meeting, and it brings
them to the meeting's door rather than into the room.

**A guest is usually somebody already on the event.** The link goes to a person or an organization in
the Directory who is listed on the event, at the e-mail address the Directory holds for them.

**Somebody who is not on the list** can be invited by name and e-mail address. At the bottom of the
Guests panel, under **Invite somebody by e-mail**, type their name and address and press **Invite**.
If somebody in the Directory already has that address, the invitation goes to them, under the name the
Directory holds. If nobody does, a person is added to the Directory with that name and address and
marked as needing review, so somebody at your firm can later keep, merge or discard the entry. Either
way they are added to the event and sent the same e-mail as anybody else, and the meeting's record
names somebody your firm can see in the Directory and in a conflicts check.

1. Open the meeting's event, or the meeting itself, and press **Guests**. It is there for anybody who
   may start meetings, once a meeting has been opened on the event.
2. The panel lists everybody on the event from outside the firm, with whether each has a link, until
   when it works, and how many times it has been used.
3. Press **Invite** beside the person you want. They are sent an e-mail with the meeting's title, its
   day and time, your firm's name and the link, and nothing else about the matter.

**Sending an organization a link** works the same way. Whoever the organization sends types their own
name when they arrive, so a client company can send whichever of its people is free.

**How long a link works.** Until two hours after the event is due to end, so a hearing that runs late
does not lock anybody out, and never longer than fourteen days from when it was sent. A link also stops
working the moment the meeting ends. It can be used to reach the door twenty times, which is plenty for
a bad connection and not enough for a link forwarded to a crowd.

**A link that went to the wrong place.** **Send a new link** replaces it: a fresh e-mail goes out and
the old link stops working at once, including for anybody in the meeting on it. **Withdraw** stops it
working without sending another, and a guest in the meeting on it leaves the room at once and is told
the firm withdrew the link. Both ask first. A withdrawn link stays on the list, marked as withdrawn,
because that somebody was invited and then was not is part of the record.

**Taking somebody off the event does not take back their link.** A link already sent keeps working
until it expires or is withdrawn, so it stays in the Guests panel under the name it was sent to,
marked **No longer on the event**, with **Withdraw** beside it for as long as it still works.

**The link on its own gets nobody into the room.** Every guest waits at the door until a host lets
them in, whatever the waiting-room switch says. At the door you see two names for each guest: the name
they typed, and underneath it the name the link was sent to. If a link for Acme Corporation arrives
under a name you do not know, that is the moment to find out who it is, before they are in the room.

**What a guest can do in the meeting**: see and hear everybody, turn their microphone and camera on
and off, share their screen, raise their hand and react, write in the meeting's chat, and leave. What
a guest writes is kept on the meeting's record like every other line, and a guest cannot take a line
back. **Polls and questions are for your firm's members**: a guest does not see Polls or the Ask as a
question box, and cannot answer a poll or ask a question. They cannot end the meeting, let
anybody in, or see anything else in Magna Carta: there is no way from their screen to any other part
of it. Everything a host can do to a member — muting, asking to unmute, spotlighting, removing —
works the same on a guest. Removing a guest, or turning one away at the door, also stops their link
bringing them back to this meeting.

## Joining as a guest

This part is for the person outside the firm who was sent a link.

1. **Open the link from the e-mail.** You see the meeting's title, its day and time and the firm's
   name. Opening it does nothing else, so it does not matter if your mail program opened it first or
   you click it twice. If the link does not open, the e-mail also carries it as text you can paste
   into your browser.
2. **Check your name.** It starts as the name the firm invited. If the link was sent to your company,
   or you go by another name, change it to yours. The host sees what you type.
3. **Check your microphone and camera.** Choose which to use, and look at the preview. Say something:
   the line under the preview says whether your microphone is picking you up. Your browser will ask
   permission first; if you say no you can still join and listen.
4. **Press Join.** You wait at the door until somebody at the firm lets you in. Nothing from your
   microphone or camera is sent while you wait, and nobody inside can see or hear you. The screen says
   how many people are ahead of you, if anybody is.
5. **Once you are let in** you are in the meeting: the people in it, the controls for your
   microphone, camera and screen, the meeting's chat, and **Leave**. What you write in the chat is
   kept by the firm as part of the meeting's record.

If you leave, or your connection drops, the same link takes you back to the door until it stops
working. If the firm withdraws your link while you are in the meeting, you leave the room and the page
says the firm withdrew the link; there is nothing to rejoin, and the firm can send you a new one. You come back to the door even if you had already been let in, and a host lets you in
again. If the meeting is locked when you arrive, the page says so and you can try again once the
host opens it.

**A link stops working once the event's time has passed**: two hours after the event is due to
end. If you still need to join after that, the firm can send you a new link.

**When a link does not work** the page says why: it has expired, the firm has withdrawn it or sent a
newer one, it has been used too many times, or the meeting is over. The firm can send you a new link
in each case. If the page itself goes wrong, it says so and gives an Error ID the firm can quote;
reload the page, or ask the firm for a new link. A link that was forwarded to you gets you only as far as the door; the host sees the name it
was sent to beside yours and decides whether to let you in.

## Recording a meeting

A meeting on a matter can be recorded. Only the sound is recorded, never the picture or anything
shared on screen.

**Who can record.** The **Record** control is in the control bar for somebody who holds the
permission to record meetings and is hosting this one. Anybody hosting the meeting can stop a
recording, whether or not they could have started it. Everybody else in the room sees whether a
recording is running and has no control over it. A meeting on a firm event cannot be recorded, because
a recording is kept on a matter; there the control is shown disabled with a line saying so.

**Starting.** Press **Record**. A panel asks you to confirm, and says that everybody in the room,
guests included, will be told. Press **Record** in the panel to start. If your firm has not switched
recording on, **Record** is shown disabled with the line "Recording is not switched on for this firm
yet." and nothing can be started (see [Your firm's meeting settings](#your-firms-meeting-settings)). The panel says so if the meeting is already being recorded, or if
there are too many people speaking in the room for one recording to hold.

**What everybody is told.** The moment a recording starts, everybody in the room hears a short tone,
hears the words "Recording in progress" where their browser can speak them, and sees a banner saying
who started it. Somebody using a screen reader is told the same words. Anybody who joins while the
recording runs is told the same way as they arrive. The banner can be dismissed; the **Recording**
mark beside the meeting's title cannot, and stays for as long as the recording runs. If your
connection drops and you come straight back into the same recording, the banner and the mark return
but the tone does not sound a second time. If you join from a calendar event or from the Meetings
page, the press on **Join** is what lets your browser play the tone; if you arrive some other way,
such as by reloading the page, some browsers (Safari among them) stay silent until you next press
something in the room, and you are told by the banner alone until then.

**Stopping.** Press **Stop Recording** in the control bar. Everybody hears one lower tone, is told
"Recording stopped", and the mark goes. Ending the meeting stops the recording too.

**After a recording stops.** The recording is put together into one audio file, with everybody who
spoke while it ran on it, guests included, and added to the matter's documents. It arrives in the
matter's **Files** the way an upload does: it is checked by the same safety scan as anything else
added to the matter, it cannot be opened until the check has finished, and if the check holds it,
it stays held like any other held file. Putting a recording together takes a little while after it
stops; a long meeting takes longer. Only the sound is in it.

**What has been recorded.** **Recordings** at the top of the meeting's screen lists every recording
made in it, newest first: what has become of it, when it started and who started it, and when and how
it stopped. The same list opens from **Recordings** on the meeting's row under **Recently ended** on
the Meetings page, so a recording can be found after the meeting is over without going back into the
room. Somebody hosting meetings can open a recording's row in the room to see who was told about it
and when: everybody who was in the room when it started, everybody who arrived while it ran, and
everybody who was there when it stopped. That list is the firm's record that people were told.

What each recording's state means:

- **Recording** — it is running now.
- **Being Put Together** — it has stopped and is being made into one audio file. Nothing is lost
  while it waits; it moves on by itself.
- **Added to the Matter** — it is in the matter's Files. Once the safety check has finished,
  **Open** in its row opens that file in a new tab, where your browser plays it; it is the matter's
  own document, not a copy, so it is also found, downloaded and shared from the Files section like
  any other document. Where it cannot be opened, the row says why instead: **Being Checked** while
  the check runs, **Held** when the check held it, **Replaced by a Later Version** when somebody has
  since added a newer version of that file, **Removed from the Matter** when the file is in the
  trash, and **Not Available** when it cannot be reached from here.
- **Failed** — it could not be put together, or nothing at all was captured, so no document was
  added. The row shows an **Error ID**; quote it to your administrator or to support.
- **Interrupted** — the recording was cut off partway through, without anybody stopping it, and
  nothing from it was added to the matter. Its row says so in its own words.

How a recording stopped is said in its own words: who pressed **Stop Recording**; "The meeting
ended"; "The sound was lost", when every voice it was hearing dropped and did not come back; "It
reached the longest a recording may run"; "It stopped when nobody had been heard for a while", when
nobody in the room was speaking for a long while, in which case the recording is still put together
and added to the matter; "It was interrupted, and nothing was added to the matter", for an
**Interrupted** recording; and "It could not start", when the recording was stopped before
anybody's voice was captured because it could not be begun properly. A recording that could not
start captured nothing.

### Reading a recording's transcript

Once a recording is added to the matter, a transcript of it is made: everything said while it ran,
in the order it was said, with each line under the name of the person who said it. It is made from
each person's own microphone, so a line is never put under the wrong name, and a guest's lines are
marked as a guest's. Making a transcript takes a little while after the recording reaches the
matter; a long meeting takes longer.

The **Transcript** column of the Recordings list says where each recording's transcript stands:

- **Being Made** — it is still being written. You can open it to watch; the panel turns into the
  transcript by itself when it is ready, and the list catches up at the same moment.
- **Read** — it is ready. Pressing it opens the transcript over the list.
- **Could Not Be Made** — the transcript failed. Open it to see the **Error ID**, and quote that to
  your administrator or to support. The recording itself is not affected.

In the transcript, each line carries where it was said in the recording, as minutes and seconds
from the start (with the hour in front once a recording runs past an hour), so you can find the same
moment in the audio. Where somebody could not be heard, because their connection dropped, the room
was too full for their sound to be kept, or what was captured could not be read back, the
transcript says so in a highlighted line: whose audio is missing, and for how long. A recording in
which nobody was heard at all says that nothing was heard. A long transcript arrives in parts;
**Load more lines** at the end brings the next part.

The transcript is also added to the matter's **Files**, beside the recording, as a captions file
(it ends in `.vtt`) that most media players can show alongside the audio. **Open Document** at the
top of the transcript opens that file in a new tab. It is the matter's own document, so it is also
found, downloaded and shared from the Files section like any other.

Because the transcript's lines are the same words as that file, they are shown only while the file
can be opened. If the file has been moved to the trash, is held by the safety check, or cannot be
reached from here, the transcript says **Transcript Withheld** and why: **Removed from the Matter**,
**Held**, or **Not Available**.

What a transcript says is part of the matter's record and is treated as privileged, like the
recording itself.

### Reading a recording's summary

Once a transcript is ready, a summary of it is written: a short account of what the meeting covered,
and a numbered list of the action items people agreed to. The summary is written by the platform from
the transcript, so it is a characterization of the conversation and not a record of it; the panel
says so above the text. When you need to know exactly what somebody said, read the transcript.

The **Summary** column of the Recordings list says where each recording's summary stands:

- **Being Written**: it is still being written. You can open it to watch; the panel turns into the
  summary by itself when it is ready, and the list catches up at the same moment.
- **Read**: it is ready. Pressing it opens the summary over the list.
- **Could Not Be Written**: the summary failed. Open it to see the **Error ID**, and quote that to
  your administrator or to support. The recording and the transcript are not affected.
- **Not Written**: no summary was written, and the panel says why. Either meeting summaries are
  switched off for your firm (an administrator switches them on in **Settings**, under **Meetings**),
  or AI processing is not switched on for your firm (an administrator
  switches it on in **Settings**, under **AI and the Assistant**), or nothing was said in the
  recording.

Each action item shows who it belongs to and when it is due. An owner is named only when the meeting
named a member of the firm exactly; otherwise the item says **No owner**. A due date is shown only
when somebody in the meeting stated the day, so "next Friday" is not turned into a date.

**Making a task of an action item.** Press **Create Task** on an item. Nothing is made yet: a
confirmation opens showing the task and who it will be assigned to, the item's owner or nobody. If you
do not want the task in the owner's name, untick **Assign to** before pressing **Create Task** there.
Putting a task in another member's name needs the permission to assign tasks (`tasks:assign`); without it, the box is
unavailable and the task is made with nobody's name on it. The task is made on the meeting's matter
like any other task, due on the item's date, and the item then offers **Open Task**, which takes you
to it in the matter's **Tasks** section. An item can be made into a task once: if somebody else got
there first, the item says so and links to their task.

**Writing it again.** Somebody who can host meetings sees **Re-run** at the top of the summary. It
asks first, because writing the summary again replaces its text and its action items; tasks already
made from the old items are kept. Re-run is also how a summary that seems stuck at **Being Written**
is started again.

If the transcript's captions file has been moved to the trash, is held by the safety check, or cannot
be reached from here, the summary says **Summary Withheld** and why, in the transcript's own words.
The summary is treated as privileged, like the transcript it was written from.

### If you are a guest

You are told about a recording exactly as everybody else is: the tone, the words, the banner and the
**Recording** mark beside the meeting's title for as long as it runs. A guest cannot start or stop a
recording. Your voice is in the recording while you are in the room, what you say is in its
written transcript under the name you gave at the door, and both are kept by the firm with the
matter's documents. If you do not want to be recorded, you can leave the meeting
with **Leave** at any time, and tell the firm.

## Your firm's meeting settings

Whether your firm's meetings can be recorded, summarized and captioned is decided once, for the whole
firm, in **Settings** under **Practice › Meetings**. Every switch there is off until somebody turns it
on. The page belongs to the people who hold the permission to manage meeting settings, which a firm's
owners and administrators hold; holding the permission to record a meeting is not enough to change
it. Somebody without it who opens the page is told the settings could not be read.

- **Record Meetings** lets a meeting on a matter be recorded. Everybody in the meeting is told when a
  recording starts, and so is everybody who joins while it runs, and the audio is added to the
  matter's documents. While it is off, **Record** in a meeting is shown disabled with the line
  "Recording is not switched on for this firm yet."
- **People Recorded at Once** is the most people one recording hears at the same time, from 1 to 11.
  Eleven is the most because that is as many voices as the recorder can hold in memory at once, so it
  cannot be set higher. A recording cannot be started in a meeting with more people in it than this,
  and anybody who joins after the limit is reached is not heard on the recording; the transcript says
  whose audio is missing.
- **Summarize Recorded Meetings** writes a summary and its action items from each recorded meeting's
  transcript. A summary also needs your firm's AI processing, which is set separately under **AI and
  the Assistant**. Beneath this switch the page says whether that is on, with a link to it; while it
  is off, no summary is written, whatever this switch says.
- **Live Captions** lets what is being said be shown as text while a meeting is live. A meeting does
  not have to be recorded to show captions, and captions themselves are not kept. Captions are still
  not yet shown in the meeting (see [What is not built yet](#what-is-not-built-yet)), so this switch
  changes nothing you can see until they are.

**When recording still cannot start.** Recording also needs the platform's recording service to be
set up for your firm. That is done by the platform, not by your firm. Until it is, the page says so
beneath **Record Meetings**, and no meeting can start a recording even with the switch on.

Changes are staged and saved together with **Save**, and every save is kept in the firm's audit log
with who made it and when.

## Ending a meeting

The control bar carries **End meeting** for whoever is running the meeting. It ends it for everybody:
the room closes, every tile goes, and everyone still in it is returned to the event. If you hold the
permission to start meetings but are not hosting this one, the screen says so rather than ending it —
somebody hosting it can hand it to you, and where nobody is running the room at all you can claim it
and then end it.

**Leave** and **End meeting** are different acts and it is worth keeping them apart. Leaving takes you
out of a meeting that carries on; ending closes it for the room. If the person who started the meeting
simply leaves, the others can keep talking — a meeting stays open until a host ends it, even when
everybody has stepped out of it, and the event on the calendar shows how many people are in it,
including none.

## What the attendance record shows

Once the meeting has ended, **Attendance** on its row under **Recently ended** on the Meetings page
shows who was in the room: a line per person with the time they joined and the time they left. A guest
is on it beside everybody else, under the name they gave at the door, marked as a guest and with the
name their link was sent to beside it — for example **Jane Hollis (Guest, invited as Acme
Corporation)** — so you can see months later that the person who came is not the one the link named. Somebody who dropped out and came back shows twice, which is the honest
account of what happened. When a host ends a meeting, everybody still in the room is recorded as
leaving at that one moment, and somebody a host removed is recorded as leaving when they were removed.

What the record does NOT keep is the running of the meeting: who muted whom, who was spotlighted, how
often the floor was cleared. Those are the mechanics of a conversation and they go when the room does.
Putting somebody out of a meeting, shutting the door and changing who is running the room are the
three that stay, because each of them is a thing somebody may later need to ask about.

That is the whole of it, apart from the chat, which is kept in full, and a recording's transcript
where the meeting was recorded. There is no duration you can bill from directly, and a meeting that
was not recorded keeps no note of what was said out loud — if the meeting is worth an entry on the
file, record your time and write a matter note the way you would after a meeting in a conference
room.

## What is not built yet

- **Reviewing people added from a meeting.** A guest invited by an address nobody in the Directory
  holds is added there marked as needing review, but the Directory does not yet list those entries
  for review. Somebody recorded on the event by name only, such as a judge, still shows a disabled
  **Invite**; invite them by name and address instead.
- **Captions on screen, and translation.** A recording gets a transcript and a summary once it is on
  the matter, but captions are not yet shown while the meeting runs, and the transcript is in the
  language that was spoken and is not translated. Video is never recorded.
- **Dialling in by telephone.** There is no number to call. Everybody joins from a browser.
- **Meetings that belong to no event.** Every meeting starts from something on the calendar. For an
  unscheduled meeting, open your own room: it puts the event on your calendar for you.
- **A standing room link for people outside the firm.** Your room's link works for colleagues signed
  in to the firm. A guest from outside is still invited to a particular meeting.
- **A seventh reaction.** The six are a closed set. Nothing lets you add one, and nothing here takes
  free text or an emoji — a firm's client can be watching the screen.
- **A gallery of ready-made backgrounds.** **Image** uses a picture you choose from your own
  computer; there is no built-in set to pick from yet.
